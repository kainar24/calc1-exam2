1. Absolute extrema: definition

Highest mountain = absolute maximum, deepest valley = absolute minimum. Unlike relative extrema, these compare $f(x_0)$ with every point of the interval.

Definition
Consider an interval in the domain of $f$ and a point $x_0$ in that interval.
  • $f$ has an absolute maximum at $x_0$ if $f(x_0)\ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in the interval;
  • an absolute minimum at $x_0$ if $f(x_0)\le f(x)$ for all $x$ in the interval;
  • an absolute extremum if it has either one at $x_0$.

The slide pictures show that anything can happen: on $(-\infty,\infty)$ a function may have only a min ($x^2$-like), none ($y=x$), or both. On an open interval $(a,b)$ a function that runs off to $\pm\infty$ has none, while a line on a closed $[a,b]$ has both.

2. Closed intervals: the Extreme Value Theorem

Theorem 1 (Extreme-Value Theorem)
If $f$ is continuous on a finite closed interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ has both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a,b]$.
Theorem 2
If $f$ has an absolute extremum on an open interval $(a,b)$, then it must occur at a critical point of $f$.
Procedure (closed-interval method) for continuous $f$ on $[a,b]$
  1. Find the critical points of $f$ in $(a,b)$ ($f'=0$ or $f'$ undefined).
  2. Evaluate $f$ at all the critical points and at the endpoints $a$ and $b$.
  3. The largest of these values is the absolute maximum value; the smallest is the absolute minimum value.
The three pictures on the slide: the max can be at an endpoint, at a smooth peak inside, or at a corner inside.
Lecture example: $f(x)=6x^{4/3}-3x^{1/3}$ on $[-1,1]$
  1. $f$ is continuous everywhere, so by the EVT both extrema exist on $[-1,1]$.
  2. $f'(x)=8x^{1/3}-x^{-2/3}=\dfrac{8x-1}{x^{2/3}}$: zero at $x=\frac18$, undefined at $x=0$. Critical points: $0,\frac18$.
  3. Table: $f(-1)=9$, $f(0)=0$, $f(\frac18)=6\cdot\frac1{16}-3\cdot\frac12=-\frac98$, $f(1)=3$.
  4. Absolute minimum $-\frac98$ at $x=\frac18$; absolute maximum $9$ at $x=-1$.

3. Infinite intervals

Remark. A continuous function may or may not have absolute extrema on an infinite interval. Look at the limits at $\pm\infty$ (table on the slide, for $f$ continuous everywhere):

$\lim_{x\to-\infty}f$$\lim_{x\to+\infty}f$conclusion on $(-\infty,\infty)$
$+\infty$$+\infty$absolute min, no absolute max
$-\infty$$-\infty$absolute max, no absolute min
$-\infty$$+\infty$neither
$+\infty$$-\infty$neither
Lecture example: $p(x)=3x^4+4x^3$
  1. $p\to+\infty$ as $x\to\pm\infty$ ⇒ an absolute minimum, no absolute maximum.
  2. $p'(x)=12x^3+12x^2=12x^2(x+1)$; critical points $x=-1,0$.
  3. $p(0)=0$, $p(-1)=3-4=-1$ ⇒ absolute minimum value $-1$ at $x=-1$.
Lecture example: $f(x)=x^4+4x$ on $(-\infty,\infty)$

$f\to+\infty$ at both ends ⇒ absolute min only. $f'(x)=4x^3+4=4(x^3+1)=0\Rightarrow x=-1$; minimum value $f(-1)=1-4=-3$.

4. Open intervals

Remark. On $(a,b)$, look at $f(x)$ as $x\to a^+$ and $x\to b^-$: both $+\infty$ ⇒ absolute min but no max; both $-\infty$ ⇒ absolute max but no min; one $+\infty$ and one $-\infty$ ⇒ neither.

Lecture example: does $f(x)=\frac1{x^2-x}$ have absolute extrema on $(0,1)$?
  1. $f(x)=\frac1{x(x-1)}$; as $x\to0^+$ and as $x\to1^-$ the denominator $\to0^-$, so $f\to-\infty$ at both ends.
  2. Therefore $f$ has an absolute maximum but no absolute minimum on $(0,1)$.
  3. $f'(x)=-\dfrac{2x-1}{(x^2-x)^2}$: critical points $x=\frac12$ (and $x=0$, which is not in $(0,1)$).
  4. The absolute maximum occurs at $x=\frac12$: $f(\frac12)=\frac1{1/4-1/2}=-4$.
Lecture example: $f(x)=\frac{x-2}{x+1}$ on $(-1,5]$
  1. $\lim_{x\to-1^+}f(x)=-\infty$ ⇒ no absolute minimum.
  2. $f'(x)=\dfrac{(x+1)-(x-2)}{(x+1)^2}=\dfrac3{(x+1)^2}>0$: no critical points, $f$ is increasing on $(-1,5]$.
  3. So the maximum occurs at the endpoint $x=5$: $f(5)=\frac36=\frac12$.

5. More examples from the lecture

(a) $f(x)=x-2\sin x$ on $\left[-\frac\pi4,\frac\pi2\right]$
  1. $f'(x)=1-2\cos x=0\iff\cos x=\frac12\iff x=\frac\pi3$ (the only critical point in the interval).
  2. $f(\frac\pi3)=\frac\pi3-\sqrt3\approx-0.68$, $f(-\frac\pi4)=-\frac\pi4+\sqrt2\approx0.63$, $f(\frac\pi2)=\frac\pi2-2\approx-0.43$.
  3. Minimum $\frac\pi3-\sqrt3$ at $x=\frac\pi3$; maximum $\sqrt2-\frac\pi4$ at $x=-\frac\pi4$.
(b) $f(x)=1+|9-x^2|$ on $[-5,1]$
  1. Remove the absolute value: $f(x)=10-x^2$ for $|x|\le3$, $f(x)=-8+x^2$ for $|x|>3$.
  2. $f'(x)=-2x$ for $|x|<3$, $2x$ for $|x|>3$; $f'=0$ at $x=0$; $f'$ does not exist at $x=\pm3$ (corners). Critical points in $(-5,1)$: $0,-3$.
  3. $f(-5)=17$, $f(-3)=1$, $f(0)=10$, $f(1)=9$.
  4. Minimum $1$ at $x=-3$; maximum $17$ at $x=-5$.
Find $p,q$ so that $f(1)=3$ is an extreme value of $f(x)=x^2+px+q$ on $[0,2]$. Max or min?
  1. $x=1$ is inside $(0,2)$, so it must be a critical point: $f'(1)=2+p=0\Rightarrow p=-2$.
  2. $f(1)=1-2+q=3\Rightarrow q=4$, so $f(x)=x^2-2x+4$.
  3. $f(0)=4$, $f(1)=3$, $f(2)=4$ ⇒ $f(1)=3$ is the minimum value.
Piecewise: $f(x)=4x-2$ for $x<1$, $(x-2)(x-3)$ for $x\ge1$, on $\left[\frac12,\frac72\right]$ (only the statement is on the slide; the solution is mine)
  1. Continuity at $x=1$: left limit $4-2=2$, $f(1)=(-1)(-2)=2$ ⇒ continuous, so the EVT applies.
  2. $f'(x)=4$ for $x<1$; $f'(x)=2x-5$ for $x>1$, zero at $x=\frac52$. At $x=1$ the one-sided derivatives are $4$ and $-3$, so $f'(1)$ does not exist ⇒ $x=1$ is critical.
  3. Candidates: $f(\frac12)=0$, $f(1)=2$, $f(\frac52)=(\frac12)(-\frac12)=-\frac14$, $f(\frac72)=(\frac32)(\frac12)=\frac34$.
  4. Absolute maximum $2$ at $x=1$; absolute minimum $-\frac14$ at $x=\frac52$.
