1. Rolle's Theorem

Theorem (Rolle)
Let $f$ be
  1. continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$,
  2. differentiable on the open interval $(a,b)$,
  3. $f(a)=f(b)=0$.
Then there is at least one point $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f'(c)=0$. (The same proof works whenever $f(a)=f(b)$.)
Proof (as on the slide)
  • If $f$ is constant, then $f'(x)=0$ for all $x$, so any $c$ works.
  • If $f(x)>0$ somewhere, then by the Extreme Value Theorem $f$ has a maximum on $[a,b]$; it is positive, so it is not at the endpoints (where $f=0$). It is at some $c$ inside $(a,b)$. Since $f$ is differentiable there, $f'(c)=0$ (a relative extremum inside is a critical point).
  • If $f(x)<0$ somewhere, the same argument with the minimum.
Lecture example: $f(x)=x^2-5x+4=(x-1)(x-4)$ on $[1,4]$
  1. $f$ is continuous on $[1,4]$ and differentiable on $(1,4)$ (polynomial); $f(1)=0=f(4)$.
  2. By Rolle there is $c\in(1,4)$ with $f'(c)=0$.
  3. $f'(x)=2x-5=0\Rightarrow c=\frac52$.
Remark: differentiability is crucial. $f(x)=|x|-1$ on $[-1,1]$

$f$ is continuous on $[-1,1]$ and $f(-1)=0=f(1)$, but $f$ is not differentiable at $x=0\in(-1,1)$ (corner). There is no horizontal tangent anywhere: $f'(x)=\pm1$, and $f'(0)$ does not exist. Rolle's conclusion fails because a hypothesis fails.

Remark: there can be more than one $c$. $f(x)=\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$

$f(0)=f(2\pi)=0$ and $f'(\frac\pi2)=f'(\frac{3\pi}2)=0$: two points.

2. Mean-Value Theorem

Theorem (Mean-Value Theorem)
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there is at least one $c\in(a,b)$ such that $$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$ Meaning: at $c$, the tangent line is parallel to the secant line joining $A=(a,f(a))$ and $B=(b,f(b))$. In motion language: at some instant the instantaneous velocity equals the average velocity.
Proof (as on the slide)
  1. The secant line $AB$: $y=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)+f(a)$. (The slide has “$-f(a)$”, a typo; it must be $+f(a)$ so that $y(a)=f(a)$.)
  2. Define $v(x)=f(x)-y$, the vertical distance between the curve and the secant.
  3. $v$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $v(a)=v(b)=0$.
  4. Rolle: there is $c\in(a,b)$ with $v'(c)=0$, i.e. $0=f'(c)-\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. ∎
Lecture example: $f(x)=\frac14x^3+1$ on $[0,2]$
  1. Continuous on $[0,2]$, differentiable on $(0,2)$.
  2. $f'(x)=\frac{3x^2}4$; secant slope $\dfrac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac{3-1}2=1$.
  3. $\frac{3c^2}4=1\Rightarrow c^2=\frac43\Rightarrow c=\pm\frac2{\sqrt3}$.
  4. Only $c=\frac2{\sqrt3}\approx1.15$ lies in $(0,2)$: there the tangent is parallel to the secant joining $(0,1)$ and $(2,3)$.

3. Consequences (textbook §4.8, used in later sections)

  • If $f'(x)=0$ on an interval, then $f$ is constant there. (MVT between any two points.)
  • If $f'(x)=g'(x)$ on an interval, then $f=g+C$. This is why every antiderivative is “$F+C$” in §5.2.
  • The monotonicity test of §4.1 ($f'>0$ ⇒ increasing) is proved with the MVT.

4. How the MVT is used on exams

  1. Find $c$: check the hypotheses, compute the secant slope, solve $f'(c)=$ slope, keep the $c$ inside $(a,b)$.
  2. Explain why it fails: find the point of discontinuity or non-differentiability in the interval.
  3. Prove an inequality (Castro-style bonus): apply the MVT on $[0,x]$ or $[x,y]$, then bound $f'(c)$ using where $c$ lies. Full examples are in the lesson above and in Practice → “MVT inequality proofs”.
