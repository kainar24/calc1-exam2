1. The area problem

How do we find the area of the region $R$ under $y=1-x^2$ over $[0,1]$? There is no simple geometric formula. Idea: approximate the area by rectangles.

Lecture example: four rectangles for $y=1-x^2$ on $[0,1]$
  • Rectangles containing $R$ (upper sum, heights at left endpoints since $f$ is decreasing): $A\approx1\cdot\frac14+\frac{15}{16}\cdot\frac14+\frac34\cdot\frac14+\frac7{16}\cdot\frac14=\frac{25}{32}=0.78125$. This overshoots the true value.
  • Rectangles contained in $R$ (lower sum, right endpoints): $A\approx\frac{15}{16}\cdot\frac14+\frac34\cdot\frac14+\frac7{16}\cdot\frac14+0\cdot\frac14=\frac{17}{32}=0.53125$. This undershoots.
  • So $0.53125<A<0.78125$.
  • Midpoint rule (heights at the midpoints $\frac18,\frac38,\frac58,\frac78$): $A\approx\left(\frac{63}{64}+\frac{55}{64}+\frac{39}{64}+\frac{15}{64}\right)\frac14=\frac{172}{64}\cdot\frac14=0.671875$. Closer to the truth, because overshoot and undershoot roughly balance. (Exact value: $\frac23$.)

Taking more and more, thinner and thinner rectangles (16, 100, …) gives better and better approximations.

2. The rectangle method

Rectangle method for finding area
  1. Divide $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals.
  2. Over each subinterval construct a rectangle from the $x$-axis up to any point on the curve above that subinterval.
  3. Let $A_n$ = total area of the $n$ rectangles. Then $A\approx A_n$.
  4. As $n$ increases the approximations get better and approach the exact area as a limit: $$A=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n.$$
Lecture example: area under $y=x^2$ over $[0,1]$
  1. Divide $[0,1]$ into $n$ subintervals of width $\frac1n$: points $0,\frac1n,\frac2n,\dots,1$.
  2. Heights at the right endpoints: $\left(\frac1n\right)^2,\left(\frac2n\right)^2,\dots,1^2$.
  3. $A_n=\left[\left(\frac1n\right)^2+\left(\frac2n\right)^2+\dots+1^2\right]\frac1n$.
  4. Numerically: $A_4=0.46875$, $A_{10}=0.385$, $A_{100}=0.33835$, $A_{1000}=0.333834$, $A_{100000}=0.333338$, so $A\approx0.3338$.
  5. Exactly (with $\sum k^2$ from §5.4): $A_n=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\to\frac13$.

3. Antiderivative method: area and derivative

Relationship between area and derivative
Suppose $f$ is nonnegative and continuous on $[a,b]$, and let $A(x)$ denote the area under the graph of $f$ over $[a,x]$, for $x\in[a,b]$. Then $$A'(x)=f(x).$$ Why: $A(x+h)-A(x)$ is a thin strip, almost a rectangle of width $h$ and height $f(x)$: $A(x+h)-A(x)\approx f(x)h$. So $\dfrac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}\approx f(x)$, and letting $h\to0$ gives $A'(x)=f(x)$.
Lecture examples: checking $A'=f$ with geometry
  • $f(x)=2$ on $[-1,x]$: rectangle, $A(x)=2(x+1)=2x+2$, so $A'(x)=2=f(x)$.
  • $f(x)=x+1$ on $[-1,x]$: triangle, $A(x)=\frac12(x+1)(x+1)=\frac12x^2+x+\frac12$, so $A'(x)=x+1=f(x)$.
  • $f(x)=2x+3$ on $[-1,x]$: trapezoid with parallel sides $1$ and $2x+3$, width $x+1$: $A(x)=\frac12\left((2x+3)+1\right)(x+1)=x^2+3x+2$, so $A'(x)=2x+3=f(x)$.
Lecture example: area under $y=x^2$ over $[0,1]$ by the antiderivative method
  1. Let $A(x)$ = area under $f(x)=x^2$ over $[0,x]$. Then $A'(x)=x^2$.
  2. So $A(x)=\frac13x^3+C$.
  3. $A(0)=0$ (no area over $[0,0]$) ⇒ $C=0$, $A(x)=\frac13x^3$.
  4. Area over $[0,1]$: $A(1)=\frac13$, which matches the limit of rectangle sums.

This is the idea behind the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (§5.6): area can be computed with an antiderivative.
