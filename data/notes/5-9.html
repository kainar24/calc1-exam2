1. Two methods for $\int_a^bf(g(x))g'(x)\,dx$

Indefinite substitution (§5.3): $\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx\ \xrightarrow{u=g(x),\ du=g'(x)dx}\ \int f(u)\,du$. For a definite integral there are two ways.

Method 1: antiderivative first, then FTC
Evaluate the indefinite integral $\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx$ by substitution, go back to $x$, then use the original limits: $$\int_a^bf(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\left[\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx\right]_a^b.$$
Method 2: substitute directly and change the limits
Put $u=g(x)$, $du=g'(x)\,dx$, and use $u=g(x)$ to replace the $x$-limits: $$\int_a^bf(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du.$$ No need to return to $x$. This is the method used in the official exam solutions.

2. Lecture examples

$\int_0^2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx$ by both methods

Method 1. $u=x^2+1$, $du=2x\,dx$: $\int x(x^2+1)^3dx=\frac12\int u^3du=\frac{u^4}8+C=\frac{(x^2+1)^4}8+C$. Then $\left[\frac{(x^2+1)^4}8\right]_0^2=\frac{625}8-\frac18=78$.

Method 2. $u=x^2+1$; $x=0\Rightarrow u=1$, $x=2\Rightarrow u=5$: $\frac12\int_1^5u^3du=\left[\frac{u^4}8\right]_1^5=\frac{625}8-\frac18=78$.

$\int_0^{\pi/8}\sin^5(2x)\cos2x\,dx$
  1. $u=\sin2x$, $du=2\cos2x\,dx$.
  2. $x=0\Rightarrow u=0$; $x=\frac\pi8\Rightarrow u=\sin\frac\pi4=\frac1{\sqrt2}$.
  3. $\frac12\int_0^{1/\sqrt2}u^5du=\frac12\left[\frac{u^6}6\right]_0^{1/\sqrt2}=\frac12\cdot\frac1{6(\sqrt2)^6}=\frac1{96}$.
$\int_2^5(2x-5)(x-3)^9\,dx$ (leftover $x$ in terms of $u$)
  1. $u=x-3$, $du=dx$, and $2x-5=2(u+3)-5=2u+1$.
  2. $x=2\Rightarrow u=-1$; $x=5\Rightarrow u=2$.
  3. $\int_{-1}^2(2u+1)u^9du=\int_{-1}^2(2u^{10}+u^9)\,du=\left[\frac{2u^{11}}{11}+\frac{u^{10}}{10}\right]_{-1}^2$.
  4. $=\left(\frac{2^{12}}{11}+\frac{2^{10}}{10}\right)-\left(-\frac2{11}+\frac1{10}\right)=\frac{52233}{110}\approx474.8$.
$\int_0^{\ln3}e^x(1+e^x)^{1/2}\,dx$
  1. $u=1+e^x$, $du=e^xdx$; $x=0\Rightarrow u=2$, $x=\ln3\Rightarrow u=4$.
  2. $\int_2^4u^{1/2}du=\frac23u^{3/2}\Big|_2^4=\frac23\left(8-2\sqrt2\right)=\frac{16-4\sqrt2}3$.
The one rule you must not break
In Method 2 the new limits belong to $u$. Never put $x$-limits on a $du$ integral, and never change the limits and then also substitute back to $x$.
