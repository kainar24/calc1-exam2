1. Increasing, decreasing, constant

Definition
Let $f$ be defined on an interval, and let $x_1,x_2$ be any two points of the interval.
  • $f$ is increasing on the interval if $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$ whenever $x_1<x_2$;
  • $f$ is decreasing if $f(x_1)>f(x_2)$ whenever $x_1<x_2$;
  • $f$ is constant if $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$ for all $x_1,x_2$.
Geometrically: increasing means the graph goes up from left to right.
Theorem (derivative test for monotonicity)
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
  • If $f'(x)>0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is increasing on $[a,b]$.
  • If $f'(x)<0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is decreasing on $[a,b]$.
  • If $f'(x)=0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a,b]$.
Picture: every tangent line has positive slope ⇒ the graph rises. Note that the conclusion holds on the closed interval (endpoints included), because $f$ is continuous there.
Lecture example: $f(x)=3x^4+4x^3-12x^2+2$, where is $f$ increasing / decreasing?
  1. $f'(x)=12x^3+12x^2-24x=12x(x^2+x-2)=12x(x+2)(x-1)$.
  2. Zeros of $f'$: $x=-2,0,1$. They split the line into four intervals. Sign table of the factors $(12x)(x+2)(x-1)$:
    intervalsigns$f'$conclusion
    $x<-2$$(-)(-)(-)$$-$decreasing on $(-\infty,-2]$
    $-2<x<0$$(-)(+)(-)$$+$increasing on $[-2,0]$
    $0<x<1$$(+)(+)(-)$$-$decreasing on $[0,1]$
    $x>1$$(+)(+)(+)$$+$increasing on $[1,\infty)$
  3. “What is special about $x=-2,0,1$?” These are exactly the points where $f$ switches between rising and falling, i.e. the candidates for relative extrema (this leads to §4.2).

2. Concavity

Concavity is the direction of curvature.

  • Concave up: upward curvature; the tangent lines have increasing slopes and the graph lies above its tangent lines (“cup”).
  • Concave down: downward curvature; tangent slopes decrease and the graph lies below its tangent lines (“cap”).
Definition (concave up / down)
If $f$ is differentiable on an open interval, $f$ is concave up there if $f'$ is increasing on the interval, and concave down if $f'$ is decreasing on the interval.
Theorem (second derivative test for concavity)
Let $f$ be twice differentiable on an open interval $(a,b)$.
  • If $f''(x)>0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is concave up on $(a,b)$.
  • If $f''(x)<0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is concave down on $(a,b)$.
Why: $f''=(f')'$, so $f''>0$ means $f'$ is increasing, which is the definition of concave up.
Lecture example: $f(x)=x^2-4x+3$

$f'(x)=2x-4$, $f''(x)=2>0$ everywhere ⇒ concave up on $(-\infty,\infty)$.

Lecture example: $f(x)=x^3$

$f''(x)=6x<0$ for $x<0$ and $>0$ for $x>0$ ⇒ concave down on $(-\infty,0)$, concave up on $(0,\infty)$. At $x=0$ the concavity switches; this is the motivating example of an inflection point.

3. Inflection points

Definition (point of inflection)
If $f$ is continuous on an open interval containing $x_0$ and $f$ changes the direction of its concavity at the point $(x_0,f(x_0))$, then $f$ has an inflection point at $x_0$.

Remark. Inflection points mark the places on $y=f(x)$ where the rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ switches from increasing to decreasing (or the other way). In other words, $f'$ has a relative max or min there.

How to find them: compute $f''$, find where $f''=0$ or $f''$ is undefined (candidates), then check that the sign of $f''$ really changes there and that $f$ is continuous there.

Lecture example: $f(x)=x^3-3x^2+1$, full analysis
  1. $f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)$. Signs: $x<0$: $(-)(-)=+$; $0<x<2$: $(+)(-)=-$; $x>2$: $+$. So $f\nearrow$ on $(-\infty,0]$, $\searrow$ on $[0,2]$, $\nearrow$ on $[2,\infty)$.
  2. $f''(x)=6x-6=6(x-1)$: negative for $x<1$ (concave down on $(-\infty,1)$), positive for $x>1$ (concave up on $(1,\infty)$).
  3. Concavity changes at $x=1$; $f(1)=1-3+1=-1$. Inflection point $(1,-1)$.
Lecture example: $f(x)=xe^{-x}$
  1. $f'(x)=e^{-x}-xe^{-x}=(1-x)e^{-x}$. Since $e^{-x}>0$: $f\nearrow$ on $(-\infty,1]$, $\searrow$ on $[1,\infty)$.
  2. $f''(x)=-e^{-x}-(1-x)e^{-x}=(x-2)e^{-x}$: concave down on $(-\infty,2)$, concave up on $(2,\infty)$.
  3. Inflection point $(2,f(2))=(2,2e^{-2})$.
Lecture example: $f(x)=x^4$ (a warning)

$f'=4x^3$, $f''=12x^2$. $f''>0$ for $x>0$ and for $x<0$, so $f$ is concave up on both sides of $0$. There is no change in concavity and hence no inflection point at $x=0$, even though $f''(0)=0$.

4. More examples (end of the lecture section)

Lecture example: $f(x)=x^3\ln x$, $x>0$
  1. Domain is $x>0$ only (that is why $x<0$ is excluded).
  2. $f'(x)=3x^2\ln x+x^3\cdot\frac1x=x^2(3\ln x+1)$. Since $x^2>0$: $f'>0\iff\ln x>-\frac13\iff x>e^{-1/3}$. So $f$ is decreasing on $(0,e^{-1/3}]$ and increasing on $[e^{-1/3},\infty)$.
  3. $f''(x)=6x\ln x+3x+2x=x(6\ln x+5)$. (The slide labels this as $f'$ and writes $6x[6\ln x+5]$, which is a typo; the sign analysis is the same.)
  4. $f''>0\iff x>e^{-5/6}$: concave down on $(0,e^{-5/6})$, concave up on $(e^{-5/6},\infty)$; inflection point at $x=e^{-5/6}$.
Lecture example: $f(x)=e^{-x^2/2}$ (also in the Exam 2 review)
  1. $f'(x)=-xe^{-x^2/2}$: increasing on $(-\infty,0]$, decreasing on $[0,\infty)$.
  2. $f''(x)=(x^2-1)e^{-x^2/2}$: concave up on $(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,\infty)$, concave down on $(-1,1)$.
  3. Two inflection points at $x=\pm1$ (points $(\pm1,e^{-1/2})$).

Summary: the procedure the lecture uses every time

  1. Find the domain.
  2. Compute $f'$ and factor it. Exponentials are positive, so drop them from the sign analysis.
  3. Make a sign table of the factors ⇒ intervals of increase/decrease (closed brackets allowed).
  4. Compute $f''$, factor, sign table ⇒ concavity (open intervals).
  5. Inflection points = points where the sign of $f''$ changes (and $f$ is continuous). Give them as points $(x_0,f(x_0))$.
